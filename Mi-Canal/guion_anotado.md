## GANCHO

In June of 2022, inflation in America hit 9.1 percent. The highest in more than forty years.

Since then, it has come way down. [VERIFICAR] The headlines say so. The Federal Reserve is basically throwing a party.

And yet you walk into a grocery store, load up your usual cart, and the total makes you wonder if somebody else's stuff got mixed in with yours.

So what is going on? Did nobody ever explain what that word actually means?

There are six reasons your wallet and the headlines disagree. Some are boring. One is a little scary. The last one changes what you should be asking for.

## BLOQUE 1

Number one. Inflation is a speed, not a price.

Think about your car. The speedometer tells you how fast you are going. The odometer tells you how far you have gone. Inflation is the speedometer. Your grocery bill is the odometer.

When the news says inflation dropped, it means prices are rising more slowly. It does not mean prices went back down. You tapped the brakes. You are still moving forward. And the odometer does not run backward.

Here is the part that surprised me. Add up a few years of "slow" and you get a big number. A basket of stuff that cost one hundred dollars in 2019 cost about one hundred twenty-three dollars in 2024. [VERIFICAR]

Twenty-three percent more, and no single year felt like a disaster on paper.

So the first mini-lesson: slower inflation is not cheaper prices. It is a smaller increase on top of a bill that already jumped.

## BLOQUE 2

Number two. The people in charge want prices to go up. Every year. On purpose.

The Federal Reserve has an official inflation target. Not zero. Two percent. It has said so openly since 2012.

Why? Because zero is dangerous. If people think prices are about to fall, they stop buying. Why get a couch today if it will be cheaper next month? Stores sell less, so they cut jobs, so people have less money, so they buy even less. Economists call that a deflation spiral, and they fear it the way you fear an email that starts with "We need to talk."

So the whole system has a small, steady price increase built in. Two percent sounds tiny. But there is a trick called the rule of seventy. Divide seventy by the rate, and you get the years it takes for something to double. Seventy divided by two is thirty-five.

At the "everything is fine" rate, prices double in about thirty-five years.

Mini-lesson: rising prices are not a glitch. They are the plan. The only fight is over how fast.

## BLOQUE 3

Number three. Prices go up like a rocket and come down like a feather.

Economists have studied this with gasoline. When the price of oil jumps, pump prices jump within days. When oil falls, pump prices drift down so slowly you could grow a beard waiting. There is even a name for it: rockets and feathers.

And it is not only a gas station conspiracy. For many businesses, changing a price is a hassle. Menus get reprinted. Contracts get renegotiated. Customers get annoyed. So companies raise prices fast when their costs spike, and lower them slowly, if at all. Once you get used to a number, that number becomes normal.

Now, a twist inside the twist. Some prices do fall. Remember the egg shortage in early 2025? Eggs hit about six dollars a dozen, and a few months later they dropped sharply. [VERIFICAR] Why? Because one specific problem, a bird flu outbreak, got better.

Specific problems can reverse. But a general jump in prices across the whole economy? That does not reverse on its own.

Mini-lesson: a spike with one cause can fade. A broad rise in the whole price level mostly stays.

## BLOQUE 4

Number four. The official number is an average. You are not average.

Inflation is measured with something called the consumer price index. Picture a giant shopping cart: housing, food, gas, medical care, clothes, cars, and more. Government statisticians check what is in that cart every month and see how much the total changed.

The biggest item in that cart is not food. It is housing. Rent and the cost of owning a home are roughly a third of the whole index. [VERIFICAR]

Now think about your own life. If you rent and your landlord just raised your rent, the cart says one thing and your bank account says another. If you drive a lot, gas matters more. If you have a baby, diapers and formula matter more. If you own your home outright, the housing part barely touches you.

So your personal inflation could be five percent while the official number says three. Or the other way around.

Mini-lesson: the headline number is a national average. Your own number can look nothing like it.

## BLOQUE 5

Number five. Your brain keeps score of the things you buy most often.

Think about what you buy every week. Milk. Eggs. Bread. Gas. Coffee. Now think about what you buy every five years. A TV. A laptop. A mattress.

TVs, by the way, have gotten far cheaper over the decades. [VERIFICAR] And when a new TV costs the same as the old one but has a better screen, statisticians count that as a price drop. [VERIFICAR] Your landlord does not accept a nicer TV as rent.

But you do not meet a TV at the register fifty times a year. You meet the eggs.

Researchers who study how people sense prices have found that we remember the prices of things we buy often, and those prices shape how we feel about inflation much more than rare purchases do. [VERIFICAR]

And in this case, your eyes are not lying. Grocery prices climbed by roughly a quarter between 2019 and 2024. [VERIFICAR]

So you get both at once: a real increase, plus a weekly reminder.

Mini-lesson: groceries are not just a price. They are a weekly alarm clock. That is why they feel worse than the headline.

## BLOQUE 6

Number six. Prices move first. Paychecks move later.

When costs jump, companies raise prices right away. Raises take longer. Reviews happen once a year. Contracts last for years. Your boss is working with a budget that was approved before any of this happened.

So there is a stretch where prices are up and your pay is not. Economists call the result "real wages," which is a fancy way of saying what your paycheck can actually buy.

In 2021 and 2022, real wages fell for a lot of workers. By the middle of 2023, on average, pay growth got ahead of inflation again. [VERIFICAR]

Great. But matching the new pace is not the same as making up the ground you lost. If you fell behind during the sprint, running at the new speed does not bring you back.

Mini-lesson: the pain is not only that prices rose. It is that your income showed up late.

## GIRO FINAL

Now here is the twist.

Suppose you got your wish. Suppose prices fell all the way back to where they were in 2019. Sounds amazing. Let me show you what that looks like in history.

Between 1929 and 1933, prices in the United States fell by roughly a quarter. [VERIFICAR] Everything got cheaper. Bread. Shoes. Haircuts. And unemployment reached about one in four workers. [VERIFICAR]

Bread was cheaper. Nobody had money for bread.

When prices fall across the whole economy, it usually means people are not spending, businesses are shrinking, and paychecks are getting cut or disappearing.

So what you want is not lower prices. What you want is a paycheck that grows faster than your own cart.

And that is the question to ask the next time you read an inflation headline. Not "Are prices going back down?" They almost certainly will not. Ask instead: "Is my pay growing faster than the things I actually buy?"

Here is a way to check. Take your last raise. Say it was four percent. Then look at your receipts from the past twelve months, groceries, rent, gas, insurance, and see how much your own cart went up. If your pay rose four percent and your cart rose eight, you did not get a raise. You got a pay cut with a thank-you note.

## CIERRE

So here is the whole mystery in one line. Inflation is the speedometer. Your grocery bill is the odometer. One slowed down. The other will never go backward.
